HOW HANUMANKIND'S
"BIG DAWGS"
PUT INDIA ON THE
GLOBAL HIP HOP MAP

10 min read
IHH Rising

There are moments in music where everything shifts — a song drops and suddenly the entire conversation changes. For Indian hip hop, that moment arrived in mid-2024 when a then-relatively unknown rapper from Kerala named Sooraj Cherukat, performing as Hanumankind, released "Big Dawgs." What followed was unlike anything IHH had witnessed before.

Within weeks, the track had crossed 50 million streams. International media outlets — the same ones that had largely ignored Indian hip hop for years — were running features. And Hanumankind was getting calls from management teams in Los Angeles.

Who is Hanumankind?

Before "Big Dawgs," Hanumankind was a respected name within India's underground circuit. Born in Kerala and later moving between cities, he developed a style that drew from Southern Indian musical traditions while absorbing global hip hop influences — from Atlanta trap to UK drill. His delivery is distinct: fast, precise, and deeply rhythmic in a way that feels uniquely desi without ever sounding like a replica of Western rap.

He had released solid music before. "Run It Up" had gathered a loyal following. But "Big Dawgs" was different from the first second. It had a confidence that announced itself before a single word was rapped.

"I wasn't trying to make a song for a global audience. I was making a song for myself. The world deciding to come along — that was just the result of it being real."

— Hanumankind, on Big Dawgs

What Makes "Big Dawgs" Different?

The Production

Producer Kalmi built a beat that is simultaneously minimal and enormous. The bass sits low and heavy — the kind of low-end that you feel in your chest at a show. But the top end is sparse, leaving room for Hanumankind's voice to occupy the full sonic space. It doesn't sound like a Bollywood-influenced beat, nor does it sound like a direct Atlanta replica. It sounds like IHH with complete confidence in its own identity.

The Flow and Delivery

Hanumankind switches cadences within verses in a way that keeps you slightly off-balance — never letting the ear settle too comfortably. He raps in English, but the rhythm of the lines carries the syncopation patterns of Malayalam and Tamil speech. It is, in the truest sense, an Indian flow that doesn't need to explain or justify itself.

The Lyricism

The bars aren't abstract poetry. They are direct, self-assured, and rooted in lived experience. There is no attempt to sound American, no code-switching for an international palate. The confidence of someone who knows exactly who he is radiates from every line.

Why This Track Broke Through — Key Factors

  • A production identity that sounds uniquely IHH — no borrowed aesthetics
  • A flow rooted in South Indian speech rhythms, not imitated Western cadences
  • Algorithmic traction on Spotify and YouTube Shorts in Western markets
  • Strategic timing — dropped at a moment when global appetite for world music was at a peak
  • No label push — entirely grassroots, which gave it authenticity that money can't manufacture

The Global Impact

The numbers tell part of the story. But the more significant impact was cultural. For the first time in a sustained way, Western music listeners were discovering Indian hip hop — not through a Bollywood crossover, not through a collaboration with an American artist, but through a purely Indian rapper making purely Indian music.

Pitchfork ran a piece. Complex acknowledged it. Billboard tracked the streams. These are publications that had treated IHH as an afterthought for years. "Big Dawgs" made them pay attention — not because it tried to fit their template, but because it refused to.

More importantly, it opened doors for every other IHH artist. When Hanumankind's name started appearing in Western feeds, curious listeners followed the trail — to Seedhe Maut, to DIVINE, to EPR, to Brodha V. The entire ecosystem benefited from a single breakout moment.

What This Means for IHH

The Indian hip hop industry has always had exceptional talent. What it has lacked — until recently — is a globally visible proof point. A song that could sit in an international playlist and hold its own. "Big Dawgs" is that proof point.

This matters for three reasons. First, it demonstrates to labels, brands, and international booking agents that IHH artists can reach global audiences. Second, it gives a generation of young Indian rappers a concrete evidence that making unapologetically Indian music is not a barrier to global reach — it is the advantage. Third, it creates a new benchmark for production quality in the scene.

"Big Dawgs proved that you don't have to sound American to be heard globally. You just have to be undeniably yourself."

— BEBAAK Editorial

The era of Indian rappers trying to replicate American sounds to get international attention is over. Hanumankind didn't borrow anything. He built something. That is the lesson every IHH artist should take from this moment.

The Road Ahead

Hanumankind's story is still being written. Since "Big Dawgs" he has performed internationally, collaborated with producers outside India, and grown an audience that extends well beyond the country's borders. More importantly, he has maintained creative control and stayed rooted in the musical identity that made the track what it was.

For BEBAAK, his story is the clearest example of what we believe about Indian hip hop: that it is not a regional variation of a global genre. It is its own thing. A genre that has earned its place on the world stage — not by asking permission, but by being undeniably excellent.

The cipher stays open. The world is listening now.
